Lab 9 · Training

Watch a language model learn

Every language model starts as random numbers. Training shows it text, scores how badly it predicts what comes next, and nudges every weight to do a little better. Train a small one here and see what it learns, what it memorises, and what that means for fine-tuning.

1. How one training step works

The model on this page reads the last few characters and predicts the next one. Large language models predict tokens instead of characters and use a transformer instead of this small network. They are trained with the same loop, repeated over trillions of tokens.

1PredictTake a batch of positions in the text. For each one, the model gives every possible next character a probability.
2ScoreThe loss is minus the log of the probability given to the character that really came next. Lower is better.
3Assign blameBackpropagation works out how much each weight pushed the loss up or down. That is the gradient.
4NudgeThe optimiser moves every weight a small step against its gradient. The learning rate sets the size of that step.

Under the hood: each character has a learned list of numbers called an embedding. The embeddings of the last few characters go through one hidden layer, which scores every character in the vocabulary. A softmax turns the scores into probabilities. The optimiser is Adam. Everything runs in your browser in plain JavaScript.

5. Memorising is not learning

Give a large model a small dataset and train it for long enough, and it stops learning general patterns. It starts storing the training text itself. Training loss keeps falling towards zero. Validation loss turns round and climbs, because the model grows confident about continuations that only exist in its training text.

The overfitting demo keeps only the first 1,500 characters and gives the model about 35,000 weights, roughly 26 for every training character. Within a few hundred steps the samples turn into long passages copied from the training text, and the memorisation meter climbs.

Takes a few seconds.

What fixes it

  • Stop at the lowest validation loss. This is called early stopping. Keep the weights from the best step, not the last one.
  • Use more, and more varied, data. Slide the character limit back up and the same model stops memorising.
  • Train fewer weights. A smaller model has less room to store the text. Fine-tuning methods that train only small adapter layers do the same.
  • Make fewer passes. The epochs counter shows how many times the model has seen each character. Dozens of passes over a small dataset is a recipe for memorising.
  • Add regularisation. Techniques such as dropout and weight decay penalise the model for leaning on any one detail. This lab leaves them out so the effect is easy to see.

6. What this means for fine-tuning

Fine-tuning is this same loop, run on a model that is already trained, with your examples as the data. Everything above applies. Three results from this lab carry straight over.

  • Patterns that repeat are learned first. Train on the FAQ preset. Within a few hundred steps every sample opens with “Q: How do I”, and by the end the answers follow the same “Open Settings, choose, then select” pattern. Format, tone and labelling schemes are what fine-tuning does well.
  • Facts are learned poorly. Read the later FAQ samples again. The format is right, but the answers often send you to the wrong menu. A fact seen once or twice is either not learned or memorised word for word, and the model cannot tell you where it came from.
  • Small datasets memorise, and memorised text comes back out. Watch validation loss, keep the number of passes low, and never train on anything you would not want the model to repeat to a user.

The options combine. A common pattern is retrieval for knowledge plus a short prompt for behaviour, with fine-tuning added later only if tests show a gap that prompting cannot close.

Engineering takeaways

  • Judge training by validation loss. Training loss almost always falls. Only held-out data that looks like production tells you whether the model learned something useful.
  • Small data plus a big model means memorising. Watch the gap between the two losses and the number of passes. Stop at the best validation step, not the last one.
  • Fine-tune for behaviour, retrieve for knowledge. Repeated patterns such as format and tone train well. Facts train badly and cannot be cited or updated without retraining.
  • Assume training data can come back out. Remove secrets, personal data and anything licensed before you fine-tune on it.
  • Build the test set first. You need it to compare prompting, retrieval and fine-tuning fairly, and to know when to stop training.
  • Sanity-check the first loss. An untrained model should score close to ln of the vocabulary size. A first loss far above that usually means a bug in the data or the set-up.
# One training step of the model on this page, in NumPy
import numpy as np

def train_step(params, X, Y, lr=0.01):
    C, W1, b1, W2, b2 = params              # X: (B, k) character ids, Y: (B,) next characters
    B = X.shape[0]
    # 1. predict
    emb = C[X].reshape(B, -1)               # look up and join the k embeddings
    h = np.tanh(emb @ W1 + b1)
    logits = h @ W2 + b2
    p = np.exp(logits - logits.max(1, keepdims=True))
    p /= p.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    # 2. score
    loss = -np.log(p[np.arange(B), Y]).mean()
    # 3. assign blame: backpropagation
    dlogits = p.copy()
    dlogits[np.arange(B), Y] -= 1
    dlogits /= B
    dW2, db2 = h.T @ dlogits, dlogits.sum(0)
    dh = (dlogits @ W2.T) * (1 - h ** 2)
    dW1, db1 = emb.T @ dh, dh.sum(0)
    dC = np.zeros_like(C)
    np.add.at(dC, X, (dh @ W1.T).reshape(B, X.shape[1], -1))
    # 4. nudge: plain gradient descent here; the page uses Adam
    for w, g in zip(params, (dC, dW1, db1, dW2, db2)):
        w -= lr * g
    return loss
